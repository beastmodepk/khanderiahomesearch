Why This Page Exists

Or: how a 1959 ranch, an imaginary second floor, and one stubborn spreadsheet started a blog.

It began with a listing

On a Friday evening in November 2026, a Redfin link landed in a chat: 3 Balsam Dr, Chelmsford — a handsome one-storey ranch, $699,900, the kind of listing you scroll past twice. The question attached to it was not "is this nice?" It was: could we add a whole second floor on top, what would that cost — and while we're at it, price the new doors, the repaint, and the kitchen counter.

That is not a question you answer with vibes. So we did the thing properly. Town bylaws were read (Chelmsford's RB district, §195-8E — genuinely more permissive than expected). Contractor pricing was gathered. Thirteen actual sold homes were lined up as comps. And the house was run through the maths twice: once as the ranch it is, once as the two-storey house it would become.

The ranch survived the maths, just about. The second floor did not: roughly $1.28 million in, for a finished house the market would value near $1.03 million. The town would likely have allowed it. The foundation might not have. The spreadsheet definitely didn't.

The problem this page solves

Every house search runs on memory, and memory is where good judgement goes to get fuzzy. Three weeks later nobody remembers why the Chelmsford house died — only that it "seemed expensive." Worse, the same mistakes queue up to be made again at the next listing: falling for the one flattering comp, pricing a renovation by hope, comparing a renovated ranch against houses that were born Colonials.

So the investigation became a page, and the page became this site. Written down, the reasoning can be checked, argued with, and reused. That is the entire point.

The rules of this blog

  1. Rentals and buying are separate hunts — separate tabs, separate maths, no mixing the two.
  2. Every serious property gets a page — listing price, photos, specs, and its town, filed under its tab and filterable by town.
  3. The numbers come before the narrative — feasibility, costs and sold comps first; feelings documented, but labelled as feelings.
  4. Parthey answers the same eight questions for every property, in the Muse chat. His answers live on the property's page. No answers, no verdict.
  5. Nothing leaves "under investigation" quietly. When a property is rejected or bought/signed, its page gets the stamp and the reason — that's when the full write-up is published.
  6. The stamp is honest. Rejected means rejected, including when the only crime was arithmetic.

What happens next

More towns will join Chelmsford in the filters as listings arrive. The Rentals tab is, at this moment, an empty room with good lighting — the first rental sent through the chat will furnish it. And House #1 sits in the Buying tab under its stamp, as a warning and a standard: the next house gets investigated at least this well.

Welcome to the search. 🏡
